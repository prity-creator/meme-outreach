# From a Pain to a Meme

## The pain chain

Every good outreach meme comes from walking one real pain forward until it turns funny:

1. **Pain** — a problem the prospect actually has. ("Our only PPC person just gave notice.")
2. **Consequence** — what it causes. ("Nobody is watching the ad accounts.")
3. **Situation** — the everyday moment it creates. ("Budget keeps spending with nobody steering.")
4. **Why it's funny** — the absurd, relatable bit. ("Slow-motion disaster nobody is looking at.")
5. **Scene** — an original, real-looking photo of people or animals that shows that feeling with no explanation.
6. **Caption** — under ~12 words, saying what the picture "is". ("Our Google Ads account the month after our PPC lead left.")

If you can't fill step 4 in one line, pick another pain. A meme that isn't funny is just a strange image.

## Concept format

Show two, recommend one:

```
Concept A (recommended): <pain in a few words>
Scene: <real-looking photo of people or animals: everyday place, one action, one emotion>
Labels (optional): <what each person, animal or object stands for>
Caption: "<under ~12 words>"
Why it fits <first name>: <one line tying it to something true about them>
```

## Does it land? (check every concept before building)

- **Specific:** would this prospect think "that's literally us"? If it could go to anyone in any industry, it's too generic.
- **True:** the pain comes from their words, a real signal, or the company brief. Not a guess.
- **One second:** the joke reads instantly on a phone screen. If it needs explaining, simplify.
- **Kind:** it laughs at the situation, never at them, their company or their work.
- **Not about us:** the meme shows the buyer's problem, never your company's solution. No product, no logo, no "we can help", no panel or sign that describes what you sell. The meme earns the reply; the pitch comes later.
- **Not about the silence:** no "when they don't reply to my DM" jokes. Those get ignored.

## Good vs. bad

| Bad (generic or off) | Good (specific and true) |
| --- | --- |
| A man holding a "please reply" sign at a window | A faded "NOW HIRING" sign in a shop window with a cobweb and a bored shopkeeper: "Our senior PPC role, month four." — for an agency with an open PPC role |
| "Marketing be like..." with a random stock photo | Sweating man pulling one block from a wobbly Jenga tower: "Increasing daily budget by 10%." — for a brand that said scaling scares them |
| A joke about their logo, website or results | A tired chef doing the books at a closed bar at night: "Me doing the books after a 14-hour Saturday." — for a restaurant owner who just opened a third location |
| A meme that explains your service | Three friends pointing at the last pizza slice labeled "Google Ads", "GA4", "CRM": "...when one customer buys." — for someone who complained about attribution |

## Real-life photo scenes

Every scene stars **real-looking people or animals** doing something with an everyday object or place. Never objects alone, never illustrations.

- One clear action and one clear emotion: sweating, head on the desk, tugging, pointing, staring in shock, sprinting.
- Ordinary people (or dogs, cats, ducks) in everyday places: kitchens, offices at night, supermarkets, restaurants, airports, garages, a lake shore.
- Labels turn people, animals or objects into the players in the joke.

## Rules

- Every meme is an original scene that looks like a real phone snapshot of people or animals. No famous meme templates, celebrities or lookalikes.
- No brand logos added to any image.
- Keep the prospect's name and company out of the image unless asked.
- Numbers in captions are part of the joke, never presented as real results.
- Seasonal pains (Q4, Black Friday, tax season, budget planning) land best a few weeks before they hit.
